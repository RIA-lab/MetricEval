# 1 Introduction

Computational enzyme design [1] now generates candidates much faster than they can be made and assayed. Diffusion models build a protein around a specified arrangement of catalytic atoms [2, 3, 4], sequence-design networks supply the sequence [5, 6], and one design case yields from tens of thousands to millions of candidates. The experiment tests about a hundred of them, one to three 96-well plates. RFdiffusion3 generated 130,000 backbones for a cysteine hydrolase, and 190 designs were tested [4]; the first round of a zinc-metallohydrolase campaign started from several hundred thousand sequences and tested 96 [7]; the Kemp-eliminase workflow produced $10^5$ to $10^6$ designs for each starting structure and tested 73 [8]. The designs that are tested are chosen by an in-silico filter, a series of metrics with cut-offs. Richter and colleagues described the task in 2011 as "analyzing, and ranking all of the produced structures to find the best handful worth experimentally characterizing" [9]. <!-- src: Butcher 2025 SI 4.1.1 (130,000 scaffolds generated; 190 designs ordered after two failed synthesis); Kim 2026 SI "Analysis and filtering of designed sequences" (several hundred thousand design sequences across hundreds of scaffolds, 96 ordered); Listov 2025 Methods (the Rosetta Match and design steps generated 10^5 to 10^6 designs for each starting structure; 73 designs tested); Richter 2011 (stage 3, quotation verbatim); other campaigns: 129 + 192 (Lauko 2025), 36 + 63 (Braun 2026), 48 to 96 per reaction (Ahern 2026), 320 (Anishchenko 2025) -->

The filter shapes the outcome. In the serine-hydrolase campaign the fraction of designs with ester-hydrolysis activity rose from 1.6% to 5.2% in the round that added a filter for the preorganization of the catalytic residues [10]. Iterative filtering of catalytic geometry on AlphaFold3 [11] predictions gave 18% multi-turnover cysteine hydrolases, where earlier one-shot campaigns gave 1–2% [4]. A second round of the metallohydrolase campaign, which added a filter on Chai-1 [12] predictions of the enzyme–substrate complex, had 11 of 96 designs above $10^3\ \mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ against 1 of 96 in the first [7]. Other things changed between rounds, so these figures show what was reported after a filter was added, not what the filter alone caused. The same filters define success when methods are compared: RFdiffusion2 yields at least one design that passes its in-silico criteria for all 41 active sites of its benchmark, RFdiffusion for 16 [3]. And passing the filter is no guarantee: of the 192 metallohydrolase designs tested, 176 have no reported kcat/KM [7]. <!-- src: Lauko 2025 main text (round 1 filtered with AF2 alone, round 2 with AF2 then PLACER; only 1.6% of AF2-passing round 2 designs passed PLACER; 2 of 129 = 1.6% and 10 of 192 = 5.2% with ester hydrolysis; probe-labelled 3% to 17%); Butcher 2025 SI 4.1 (35 of 190 = 18% multi-turnover, 73 of 190 = 38% above background; earlier campaigns 1-2%); Kim 2026 main text (11 of 96 versus 1 of 96 with kcat/KM above 10^3) and SI "Filtering with Chai-1 predictions" (Chai-1 used as a filter in the second campaign only); Ahern 2026 (at least one scaffold passing the success filters for 41/41 cases, RFdiffusion 16/41); this paper Results 3.1.2 (192 designs, 16 with a reported kcat/KM) -->

Pipelines filter on different metrics. Rosetta energy terms [13] and shape terms (ligand-binding energy, shape complementarity, contact molecular surface, hydrogen bonds) filtered luciferase and metallohydrolase designs [7, 14]; the Kemp-eliminase objective used energy density and the van der Waals energy of the catalytic base [8]. Agreement of the catalytic residues with the design is a common criterion: the side-chain RMSD of the catalytic tetrad [15], or the RMSD and distances of the catalytic residues after re-prediction [4, 7]. Structure predictors supply the rest: pLDDT, RMSD to the design and PAE of AlphaFold2 [16], and pTM, ipTM, PAE and ligand-pose RMSD of AlphaFold3 or Chai-1 models of the enzyme–substrate complex, with ensembles of side-chain conformations to score preorganization [17]. Several pipelines combine these in composite scores [4, 7, 8], with cut-offs that differ between campaigns (pLDDT thresholds run from 72.5 to 90 [4, 7]) and weights chosen by judgement, in one case "an arbitrary initial guess" [7]. Our panel covers the families that can be computed from a design model or a co-folded prediction: 15 Rosetta energy terms, 8 geometric descriptors of the catalytic residues, 6 pKa estimates for them, and 4 prediction-based metrics (interface confidence and the two active-site accuracy criteria of the RFdiffusion2 benchmark), 33 in all (Methods 2.1). <!-- src: Yeh 2023 Methods (ligand-binding energy, protein-ligand hydrogen bonds, shape complementarity, contact molecular surface); Kim 2026 SI (Rosetta ddG, contact molecular surface, SASA, hydrogen bonds; AF2 pLDDT >= 72.5, C-alpha RMSD <= 1.75 A, PAE <= 7.25, pTM >= 0.725; Chai-1 C-alpha RMSD <= 1.25 A, catalytic residue RMSD <= 1.5 A, ipTM >= 0.7, pTM >= 0.875, PAE <= 6, 4 of 5 models; composite score "weights were an arbitrary initial guess"); Listov 2025 Methods (fuzzy-logic objective: energy density, energy rank, active-site vdW, catalytic base vdW, ligand solvation, theozyme geometry); Braun 2026 (ranked predominantly by the sidechain RMSD of the catalytic tetrad); Butcher 2025 SI 4.1.1 (AF3 ensembles; motif RMSD < 1.25 then 1.0 A, pTM > 0.7 then 0.8, catalytic distances <= 3.5-4.0 A, per-residue pLDDT > 80, composite score with pLDDT >= 90 targets, minimum chain-pair PAE < 5 A, pose RMSD < 3 A); Anishchenko 2025 (PLACER ensembles, pRMSD); Methods Table 1 (panel) -->

Whether these metrics respond to catalysis is not known. A filter is in practice judged by the hit rate of the campaign that used it, and between rounds a campaign changes its scaffolds, catalytic motif, sequence design and filter together. The authors of recent campaigns say as much. The final selection of metallohydrolase designs combined many metrics instead of applying strict cut-offs "due to the lack of known standalone predictive metrics for metallohydrolase activity" [7], and the Riff-Diff study reports a "negligible correlation between predicted enzyme rankings and actual activity in this and previous studies" [15]. <!-- src: Kim 2026 SI "Filtering and analysis after PLACER" (verbatim); Braun 2026 Discussion (verbatim). Background only, not cited: the Riff-Diff peer-review file, where the authors note that with 35 designs a correlation analysis of many computational metrics against activity has limited value -->

The nearest studies address neighbouring problems. A filter developed from 20 metrics raised the experimental success of generated malate dehydrogenases and copper superoxide dismutases by 50–150% [18], but those metrics describe whole sequences and apo structures, not a ligand or catalytic residues. Quantum-mechanical and molecular-dynamics scoring was evaluated on the Kemp-eliminase designs of 2008 [19], not the fast filters that reduce $10^5$ designs to about a hundred. For binder design, a meta-analysis of 3,766 designs ranks interface metrics against binding [20] and a design competition named the lack of robust computational metrics as a key challenge [21]; for monomers, studies test whether predictor confidence tracks experimental success [22, 23]. Adversarial tests show that co-folding predictions depart from expected physical behaviour when the binding site is perturbed [24] and that AlphaFold3 structures stay invariant to mutations of up to 40% of residues [25]. To our knowledge, no study has tested the metrics used to filter structure-based de novo enzyme designs against catalysis. <!-- src: Johnson 2025 abstract (20 metrics, over 500 sequences, two families = malate dehydrogenase and copper superoxide dismutase, success +50-150%) and Results (alignment-based, alignment-free and AF2-structure-based metrics); Kiss 2010 abstract (2008 Kemp-elimination designs and inactive designs; QM, ONIOM QM/MM, AMBER MD); Overath 2025 abstract (3,766 binders, 15 targets, over 200 features); Cotet 2025 abstract (lack of standardized benchmarks, experimental design targets and robust computational metrics for method comparison); Garcia 2026 abstract (614 monomers, AlphaFold/ESMFold/ProteinMPNN confidence, moderate discrimination); Korbeld 2026 abstract (self-consistency/refolding pipeline, reliability as a filter); Masters 2025 abstract (adversarial perturbations of protein-ligand co-folding); Feldman 2026 abstract (AlphaFold3 invariant to mutations of up to 40% of residues) -->

We therefore ask whether the metrics respond to catalysis or to something simpler, such as how close a change is to the active site. Four tests, each a question that a filter has to answer, run one panel of 33 metrics through the same standard of evidence (Methods 2.1). The *activity discrimination test* asks whether a metric tells an active enzyme from an inactive one, the basic job of a filter; it uses 21 natural zymogen–mature pairs, whose precursor is the dead form, and the 192 metallohydrolase designs, 16 of them with a reported kcat/KM [7]. The *substrate discrimination test* asks whether a metric knows which substrate an enzyme is built for, the premise of ligand-conditioned design: a structure predictor is given 55 natural enzymes with their own ligand and with a wrong one. The *activity ranking test* asks whether, among enzymes that work, a better score means higher activity, which is how the last designs are chosen; it uses measured kcat/KM values of 432 variants of the β-glucosidase BglB and of the 16 active designs. The *detection ability test* asks whether a metric notices damage to the catalytic residues more than the same damage elsewhere in the protein, which separates a metric of catalysis from one of location; the catalytic residues of up to 195 natural enzymes are changed in the computer and each metric is compared with a matched non-catalytic control. The first three tests use proteins as they exist, the fourth damages them on purpose. Every test ranks the metrics against reference rows and trivial baselines, such as the distance to the catalytic residues, with a threshold that allows for ranking many metrics at once. <!-- src: Methods 2.1 to 2.5; Results 3.1 to 3.4 -->

A different metric leads each test, and in three of them the leader is matched by a trivial reference or loses its lead under a stricter control (Results 3.1 to 3.4). In the activity discrimination test the leading metric, the intra-residue repulsion of the catalytic residues (Rosetta), does not separate natural dead from active enzymes beyond chance for the best of 39 items (AUROC 0.61, against a threshold of 0.68; 0.5 means no separation), whereas the whole-prediction confidence pLDDT does (0.79); among the 192 designs it leads again (0.80), and the tyrosine fraction, which holds no chemistry, comes close (0.75). In the substrate discrimination test ipTM leads (mean AUROC 0.70), while the active-site accuracy criteria barely separate the cognate ligand from a wrong one (0.53 to 0.56). In the activity ranking test the range of the catalytic pKa leads over 432 BglB variants (Spearman ρ = 0.48), but the distance from the mutated residue to the catalytic residues reaches 0.46 and no metric beats it; among the 16 active designs polar contacts lead (ρ = 0.67). In the detection ability test the per-chain pTM minimum responds most to catalytic damage (R = 0.75, the probability that it changes more at the catalytic lesion than at the matched control; 0.5 means no specificity) and falls to 0.59 when the control lies at the same distance from the ligand; the best structure-space metric is the pKa range (0.68).
