# 1 Introduction

Computational enzyme design [@listov2026] has a throughput problem at its output, not at its input. Generative models now propose backbones and sequences without practical limit [@watson2023; @dauparas2022; @ahern2026], while the experimental channel stays narrow: a recent metallohydrolase campaign deposited 199 models and carried 192 designs onto two 96-well plates, of which 16 showed measurable activity [@kim2026]. <!-- src: data/raw/baker2025/PROVENANCE.md (199 = 96 + 7 + 96); results/P6/REPORT.md (192 plated, 16 with a reported kcat/KM) -->

Everything between the model and those few hundred wells is a metric. Candidates are ranked by Rosetta energy terms [@alford2017], by geometric criteria on the active site, by the confidence of a structure predictor [@jumper2021; @abramson2024; @chai2024] and by ensemble stability [@anishchenko2025], and the ranking decides what is made. The hit rate a campaign can reach is therefore bounded by what those filters are able to see.

What they can see has not been established. The question is old: Kiss and colleagues [@kiss2010] posed it for the first designed retro-aldolases, and the field's canonical successes were rescued by directed evolution rather than by prediction [@rothlisberger2008; @jiang2008; @siegel2010; @blomberg2013; @yeh2023; @lauko2025]. The audits since then have measured other things. They ask whether models produce foldable, soluble, expressible proteins [@garcia2026; @overath2025; @korbeld2026], or they benchmark sequence-side models that carry no geometric metric at all [@spinner2026; @zeng2026]. To our knowledge none asks whether a geometric or energetic criterion responds to *catalysis*. The active-site criterion is the most consequential omission, because it is the field's own definition of a successful design.

The experiment that tests it is a perturbation. If a metric detects catalysis, then damaging a catalytic site should move it more than the identical damage applied elsewhere in the same protein. The second half of that sentence is the whole difficulty. A metric that responds to any disturbance will respond to a catalytic one, and reporting that response as catalytic detection is a category error that no amount of statistical care repairs. We therefore pair every catalytic lesion with the same lesion at a matched non-catalytic site of the same protein, under an identical structural treatment, each arm referenced against its own treated baseline. The ratio of the two responses, the specificity ratio, is the quantity of interest. The same logic has shown that widely used predictors are insensitive to the physics they are assumed to encode: co-folding poses that stay put when the binding site is perturbed [@masters2025], and folding confidence that stays put when the sequence is perturbed [@feldman2026]. We apply it to catalysis, and to the criteria the field actually gates on.

A matched control on residue type and burial is not yet a fair control. A catalytic residue sits next to the ligand and next to the other catalytic residues, and a metric that tracks *where in the protein* a change happens will look catalytic when it is only local. We therefore ask the question four ways, in four experiments that use the same panel of 33 metrics and the same standard of evidence (Fig. 1 of the Methods). Two experiments use real proteins as they are; two introduce a deliberate change. First, we compare real catalytically dead enzymes with their active forms, on an evaluation set of 21 zymogen–mature pairs, and active designs with no active designs, on 192 plated designs. Second, we give a structure predictor the cognate substrate and then a wrong one, on 55 natural enzymes. Third, we compare the metrics with measured activity: 432 variants of one β-glucosidase and a plated design campaign. Fourth, we damage the catalytic residues of 143 natural enzymes in the computer and score the structure-space metrics against matched controls, and we repeat the damage in the sequence of re-predicted complexes with a control matched also on the distance to the ligand. The Results report them in this order.

Here we show that the metrics respond to catalytic-site damage far more readily than they separate it from other damage. On 21 real dead–active pairs, no main-set metric separates the two forms beyond what chance gives the best of 39 ranked items (highest AUROC 0.61 [0.52, 0.72]); the quantities that do are summaries of whole-prediction confidence, such as pLDDT (0.79 [0.68, 0.90]). On 192 plated designs, 16 of them active, two Rosetta repulsion terms of the catalytic residues exceed the corresponding bar (0.80 [0.64, 0.89] and 0.78 [0.57, 0.92]), and so does the tyrosine fraction (0.75 [0.55, 0.86]), a reference row that needs no catalytic information. When a structure predictor is given a wrong ligand, ipTM separates it from the cognate one with an AUROC of about 0.70, partly through the size of the ligand, and the active-site accuracy barely does (0.53 to 0.56). In 432 BglB variants the best metric reaches a rank correlation of 0.48 [0.35, 0.59] with the measured impairment, no better than the distance from the mutated residue to the catalytic glutamates alone (0.46 [0.32, 0.57]). Under matched perturbation of 143 natural enzymes, 23 of 29 structure-space metrics respond to an isosteric substitution at a catalytic residue and none responds more than to the same substitution at a matched control; specificity appears for 5 to 8 metrics, and only at lesions of 36 Å³ or more of side-chain volume. The largest ratios belong to burial descriptors (up to 2.4) and the rest to a few Rosetta terms (1.1 to 1.9). For the predictor, the specificity of interface confidence (a ratio near 2) falls to 1.18 [0.85, 1.60] once the distance to the ligand is adjusted for, and no interface term meets the pre-registered rule for specificity. Ranked by the probability that a metric changes more at the catalytic lesion than at its matched control, only three structure-space metrics exceed the best-of-33 threshold (0.63 to 0.68), and the predictor's interface terms fall from 0.67 to 0.75 to 0.35 to 0.59 once the control is matched on distance. <!-- src: tables/main/T3_dead_vs_active.csv; tables/main/T4_plated_designs_auroc.csv; tables/main/T5_substrate_swap.csv; tables/main/T6_bglb_variants.csv; tables/main/T8_specificity_structure_space.csv; tables/main/T9_specificity_predictor_ladder.csv -->

We are careful about what this does not show. Perturbation response is not activity prediction. A synthetic-negative AUROC is never a headline here. A response threshold measured on natural enzymes does not transfer to designs, which is a separate hypothesis that we do not test. Active-site accuracy against a deposited structure and against a design's own model are different metrics and are never pooled. Our contributions are an audit of which metric can be tested in which experiment, a benchmark in which every claim carries a matched control and a declared trivial baseline, and four experiments whose results can be read side by side.
