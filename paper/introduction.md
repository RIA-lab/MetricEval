# 1 Introduction

Computational enzyme design [1] now produces far more candidates than can be tested. A single design problem yields tens of thousands to millions of designs: 130,000 backbones for a cysteine hydrolase designed with RFdiffusion3 [2], several hundred thousand sequences for a zinc metallohydrolase [3], and $10^5$ to $10^6$ designs for each starting structure in a Kemp-eliminase workflow [4]. The experiment, one or two 96-well plates, takes up about a hundred, and an in-silico filter decides which. Richter and colleagues described this filtering step in 2011 as "analyzing, and ranking all of the produced structures to find the best handful worth experimentally characterizing" [5]. <!-- src: Butcher 2025 SI 4.1.1 (130,000 scaffolds generated; 190 designs ordered); Kim 2026 SI "Analysis and filtering of designed sequences" (several hundred thousand design sequences across hundreds of scaffolds; 96 ordered per round); Listov 2025 Methods (10^5 to 10^6 designs for each starting structure; 73 tested); Richter 2011, stage 3 (verbatim). Other campaigns: 129 + 192 designs (Lauko 2025), 36 + 63 (Braun 2026), 48 to 96 per reaction (Ahern 2026), 320 (Anishchenko 2025) -->

Recent campaigns have invested heavily in this step. The RFdiffusion3 cysteine hydrolases went through three rounds of filtering on AlphaFold3 [6] predictions of the catalytic geometry, and 18% of the 190 designs tested were multi-turnover enzymes, against 1–2% in earlier campaigns [2]. In the serine-hydrolase campaign, the fraction of designs with ester-hydrolysis activity rose from 1.6% to 5.2% in the round that added a filter for the preorganization of the catalytic residues [7]. In-silico criteria also decide which method is judged better: RFdiffusion2 yields a design that passes them at all 41 active sites of its benchmark, RFdiffusion at 16 [8, 9]. Filtering is thus a decisive step in enzyme design, yet passing it is no guarantee of activity: of the 192 designs tested in the metallohydrolase campaign, 176 have no reported kcat/KM [3]. <!-- src: Butcher 2025 SI 4.1 (three rounds of LigandMPNN sampling with AF3 ensembles of the first tetrahedral intermediate, filtered on motif RMSD and catalytic distances; 35 of 190 = 18% multi-turnover; earlier one-shot campaigns 1-2%); Lauko 2025 main text (round 1 filtered with AF2 alone, round 2 with AF2 then PLACER; 2 of 129 = 1.6% and 10 of 192 = 5.2% with ester hydrolysis; other changes were made between rounds, hence "in the round that added"); Ahern 2026 (at least one scaffold passing the success filters for 41/41 cases, RFdiffusion 16/41); Kim 2026 and this paper Results 3.1.2 (192 designs, 16 with a reported kcat/KM) -->

The filters themselves are far from standardized: what they measure differs from one pipeline to the next. Some rank designs by Rosetta energies and shape complementarity [3, 4, 10], some by how closely a predicted structure reproduces the intended catalytic geometry [2, 11], and others by the confidence of AlphaFold or Chai-1 models of the enzyme–substrate complex [6, 12, 13] or by the preorganization of the active site in an ensemble of predicted conformations [7, 14]. Many pipelines combine several of these into a composite score [2, 3, 4]. Cut-offs differ from campaign to campaign, and the weights are set by judgement, in one case as "an arbitrary initial guess" [3]. <!-- src: Yeh 2023 Methods (ligand-binding energy, protein-ligand hydrogen bonds, shape complementarity, contact molecular surface); Kim 2026 SI (Rosetta ddG, contact molecular surface, hydrogen bonds; AF2 pLDDT >= 72.5, Calpha RMSD <= 1.75 A; Chai-1 ipTM >= 0.7, pTM >= 0.875, PAE <= 6; weights "an arbitrary initial guess"); Listov 2025 Methods (fuzzy-logic objective: energy density, energy rank, active-site vdW, catalytic base vdW, ligand solvation, theozyme geometry); Braun 2026 (ranked predominantly by the sidechain RMSD of the catalytic tetrad); Butcher 2025 SI 4.1.1 (AF3 ensembles; motif RMSD < 1.25 then 1.0 A, pTM > 0.7 then 0.8, catalytic distances <= 3.5-4.0 A, pLDDT >= 90 targets, minimum chain-pair PAE < 5 A); Anishchenko 2025 and Lauko 2025 (PLACER ensembles, pRMSD) -->

That judgement has little evidence to draw on: it is not known whether any of these metrics responds to catalysis. A campaign judges its filter by its own hit rate, which also reflects every other change made between rounds. For the final selection, the designers of the metallohydrolases combined many metrics instead of applying cut-offs "due to the lack of known standalone predictive metrics for metallohydrolase activity" [3]. The authors of Riff-Diff, who ranked their designs mainly by the RMSD of the catalytic residues, report a "negligible correlation between predicted enzyme rankings and actual activity in this and previous studies" [11]. Systematic evaluations exist for neighbouring problems: filters for generated enzyme sequences [15], interface metrics for protein binders [16], predictor confidence for monomer designs [17, 18] and, in 2010, quantum-mechanical and molecular-dynamics scoring of the first Kemp-eliminase designs [19]. To our knowledge, none has asked whether the fast metrics that filter structure-based enzyme designs respond to catalysis. <!-- src: Kim 2026 SI "Filtering and analysis after PLACER" (verbatim); Braun 2026 Results (ranked predominantly by the sidechain RMSD of the catalytic tetrad) and Discussion (verbatim). To address this, we asked what a metric that tracks catalysis should be able to do, and turned each answer into a test. It should tell an enzyme that works from one that does not, which is what a filter is for (*activity discrimination test*). It should also know which substrate an enzyme is built for, as every design conditioned on a substrate assumes (*substrate discrimination test*), and order enzymes by their activity, because a selection ends by choosing the best of the designs that pass (*activity ranking test*). And it should notice damage to the catalytic residues more than the same damage elsewhere in the protein, since that is what separates catalysis from geometry (*detection ability test*). We applied the tests to 33 metrics computable from a design model or a co-folded prediction (Methods 2.1). The first three observe natural enzymes and designs as they are, whereas the fourth damages natural enzymes on purpose, which is what allows catalysis to be told apart from proximity to the active site. Every test ranks the metrics alongside trivial references, such as the distance to the catalytic residues, so that a metric is credited only if it does better than something simple.

In activity discrimination on natural enzymes, pLDDT ranked first (AUROC 0.79) and the best of the 33 metrics, the intra-residue repulsion of the catalytic residues, reached 0.61, against a chance threshold of 0.68 for the best of 39 items. On the designs the intra-residue repulsion ranked first (0.80) and the tyrosine fraction reached 0.75. In substrate discrimination, ipTM ranked first (0.70) and the RMSD of the catalytic atoms reached 0.53 to 0.56. In activity ranking, the range of the catalytic pKa ranked the BglB variants first (Spearman ρ = 0.48), the distance from the mutated residue to the catalytic residues reached 0.46, and no metric beat it.
