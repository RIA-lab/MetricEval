# Abstract {.unnumbered}

As designs outnumber what a laboratory can test, in-silico metrics decide which enzymes are built, yet it is not known whether they respond to catalysis or to something simpler, such as proximity to the active site. We asked what a metric that tracks catalysis should be able to do and turned each answer into a test. It should tell an enzyme that works from one that does not, the basic job of a filter (*activity discrimination*); know which substrate an enzyme is built for, as every design conditioned on a substrate assumes (*substrate discrimination*); order enzymes by activity, because a selection ends by choosing the best of those that pass (*activity ranking*); and notice damage to the catalytic residues more than the same damage elsewhere, which separates catalysis from proximity to the active site (*detection ability*, the only test that damages natural enzymes on purpose). We applied the tests to 33 metrics and, in every test, ranked them beside trivial references, such as the distance to the catalytic residues, so that a metric is credited only if it does better than something simple. In three of the four tests the best metric was matched by a simple reference or lost its advantage under a stricter control.
